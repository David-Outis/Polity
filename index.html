Milestones & Timeline

The key dates and people behind the drafting and adoption of the Constitution.

— chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (celebrated as Constitution Day / Samvidhan Diwas) — celebrated as Republic Day
EventDate / Details
Idea of a Constituent Assembly first proposed byM.N. Roy
Formation of the Constituent Assembly1946
First meeting of the Constituent AssemblyDecember 9, 1946
Provisional President of the AssemblySachchidananda Sinha
Appointment of the Drafting CommitteeAugust 29, 1947
Constitution passed by the AssemblyNovember 26, 1949
Constitution came into full operationJanuary 26, 1950
Why 26 January? The date was chosen deliberately — it was on 26 January 1930 that the Indian National Congress had first declared Purna Swaraj (complete independence) as its goal. The Constitution's commencement date honours that pledge.

Dr. Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar is known as the Father of the Indian Constitution, for his role as Chairman of the Drafting Committee and principal architect of its final text.

Section 3

Constitutional Amendments

The Constitution has been amended 106 times as of 2024. Below are the amendments most frequently asked about in exams, each with a short note on why it matters.

AmendmentYearDetails & Why it matters
1st1951Added the Ninth Schedule — laws placed here were originally shielded from judicial review, mainly to protect land reform laws from being struck down as violating the Right to Property.
12th1962Goa, Diu, and Daman admitted to the Indian Union as Union Territories, following their liberation from Portuguese rule.
13th1962Nagaland granted special status under Article 371A, protecting its customary law and land ownership practices.
21st1967Sindhi included in the 8th Schedule, raising the count of scheduled languages.
24th1971Made the President's assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill mandatory — done in response to Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967), which had held Parliament couldn't amend Fundamental Rights.
25th1971Restricted the fundamental right to property, giving Parliament more room to pursue land reform without full judicial interference.
31st1972Raised Lok Sabha seats from 525 to 545, reflecting population growth.
36th1975Made Sikkim the 22nd state of India.
42nd1976Known as the "Mini-Constitution" — added "Socialist", "Secular", and "Integrity" to the Preamble; added the 10 Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A); passed during the Emergency and later partly reversed by the 44th Amendment.
44th1978Removed the Right to Property from the list of Fundamental Rights (it became a mere legal right under Article 300A); also replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as grounds for National Emergency, to prevent future misuse.
52nd1985Introduced the Tenth Schedule — the Anti-Defection Law, disqualifying legislators who defect from their party.
69th1991Granted special status to Delhi, making it the National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi with its own Legislative Assembly.
71st1992Added Konkani, Meitei (Manipuri), and Nepali to the 8th Schedule — raising official languages to 18 at the time (now 22).
73rd1992Strengthened the Panchayati Raj system, giving rural local government constitutional status (Part IX, 11th Schedule).
93rd2005Enabled reservations for socially/educationally backward classes in educational institutions, including private unaided ones (except minority institutions).
101st2016Introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST) — a major indirect tax reform, implemented from 1 July 2017.
102nd2018Gave constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC).
103rd2019Introduced a 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) among the general category.
106th2023Reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, and the Delhi Legislative Assembly, including seats already reserved for SCs/STs — known as the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam.

Part IV-A (Article 51A) — Fundamental Duties

Added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976. There are now 11 Fundamental Duties (the 11th was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002).

ArticleDescription
Article 51A 1. Abiding by the Constitution and respecting its ideals, institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem

2. To cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the National Struggle for Freedom

3. To uphold & protect the Sovereignty, Unity, and Integrity of India

4. To defend the country and render National Service when called upon to do so

5. Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all people of India transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities; renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women

6. Value and preserve the rich heritage of the country's composite culture

7. Protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures

8. Develop scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform

9. Safeguard public property and abjure violence

10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement

11. Provide opportunities for education to one's child or ward between the ages of six and fourteen years (added by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002)
Fundamental Duties were inspired by Article 29 of the erstwhile USSR Constitution and recommended by the Swaran Singh Committee. Unlike Fundamental Rights, no legal penalty attaches directly to their breach (Parliament can, however, make laws to enforce them).

Section 26

Landmark Supreme Court Cases

Pairs naturally with Section 14 (The Judiciary). These are the cases most frequently tested — each with the one-line principle it established.

CaseYearPrinciple established
A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras1950Took a narrow view of Article 21 — "procedure established by law" only required a validly enacted procedure, not that it be fair or reasonable (later overturned by Maneka Gandhi).

Section 32

Governor's Discretionary Powers

Under Article 163, the Governor normally acts on the aid and advice of the state Council of Ministers — but the Constitution itself carves out situations where the Governor may act in their own discretion, and courts have added further limits and guidance on top.

SituationNature of discretion
Appointing the Chief Minister when no party has a clear majorityConstitutional/situational discretion — the Governor decides whom to invite to form the government (usually the single largest party or a post-poll alliance able to prove majority), subject to S.R. Bommai's requirement that the majority be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Governor's own assessment.
Dismissing a Council of Ministers that has lost majority supportSituational discretion, but only after the loss of majority is demonstrated on the floor of the House — cannot be based on the Governor's subjective satisfaction alone (per S.R. Bommai).
Dissolving the Legislative AssemblySituational discretion, generally exercised on ministerial advice, but the Governor can decline to dissolve at the request of a Council of Ministers that has already lost its majority.
Reserving a Bill for the President's consideration (Article 200)Express constitutional discretion — the Governor may reserve a Bill instead of giving assent, particularly one that could endanger the position of the state's High Court or is of a nature the Constitution wants Union scrutiny on.
Recommending President's Rule (Article 356)Discretionary — but the report is only a recommendation; the actual proclamation is by the President based on the Union Council of Ministers' advice, and is subject to judicial review per S.R. Bommai.
Seeking information from the Chief Minister on administration (Article 167)Not exactly "discretionary" but an independent constitutional right of the Governor to be informed, functioning as a check even though the Governor doesn't act unilaterally on it.
Special discretionary responsibilities in certain statesSome Governors have express special responsibilities under specific Articles — e.g. Nagaland (Art. 371A, law and order in Naga Hills-Tuensang), Maharashtra/Gujarat (Art. 371, development boards for backward regions), Arunachal Pradesh (Art. 371H, law and order), Sikkim (Art. 371F).
Administration of Sixth Schedule tribal areas (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram)The Governor has express discretion under paragraph 9 of the 6th Schedule regarding royalties from licences/leases for mineral exploration in these areas.
Article 163(2) says that if any question arises whether a matter falls within the Governor's discretion, the Governor's decision is final, and its validity cannot be questioned on the ground that they ought or ought not to have acted in their discretion — though this doesn't put the exercise of the underlying power itself entirely beyond judicial review, as S.R. Bommai and later cases have shown regarding Articles 356 and 200.

Section 44

Government of India Acts (1858–1935)

Section 1 covers the milestones of the Constitution's own drafting. This section goes further back — the pre-independence legislative history that shaped many of the Constitution's institutions, a classic SSC ask.

ActWhat it introduced
Government of India Act, 1858Ended East India Company rule after the 1857 revolt; transferred power directly to the British Crown; created the office of Secretary of State for India, assisted by a Council of India; Governor-General became "Viceroy" as the Crown's direct representative.
Indian Councils Act, 1861Began associating Indians with law-making — allowed the Viceroy to nominate Indians to his Legislative Council (in a non-official capacity); initiated the process of decentralisation by restoring legislative powers to Bombay and Madras presidencies; began the portfolio system.
Indian Councils Act, 1892Introduced the principle of indirect election ("selection") for the first time; enlarged the Legislative Councils' size and gave them the power to discuss the budget and ask questions to the executive.
Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)Introduced separate electorates for Muslims for the first time — widely seen as the origin of communal representation in Indian politics; allowed Indians to be members of the Viceroy's Executive Council for the first time (Satyendra Prasad Sinha).
Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)Introduced "Dyarchy" (dual rule) in the provinces — subjects divided into "transferred" (administered by ministers responsible to the legislature) and "reserved" (administered by the Governor and his Executive Council); introduced bicameralism and direct elections at the Centre for the first time; set up a Public Service Commission.
Government of India Act, 1935The most detailed Act before independence and the main structural template for the Indian Constitution — provided for an All-India Federation of provinces and princely states (never actually implemented); abolished provincial Dyarchy and introduced provincial autonomy instead; introduced Dyarchy at the Centre instead; established a Federal Court (1937); provided for a Reserve Bank of India; further extended separate electorates.
Indian Independence Act, 1947Ended British rule; partitioned British India into India and Pakistan as two independent dominions; abolished the office of Secretary of State for India; made the Constituent Assembly a fully sovereign body empowered to frame the Constitution and function as the interim central legislature.
The Government of India Act, 1935 is the single most-borrowed-from source for the Indian Constitution — federal scheme, office of Governor, Public Service Commissions, judiciary structure, and emergency provisions all trace back to it, which is why Section 7 (Sources of the Constitution) lists it as the largest single source.
